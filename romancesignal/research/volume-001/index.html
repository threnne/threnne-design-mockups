Volume 001 · May 2026 · Romance Signal

Off Campus and Heated Rivalry.
What the Trad Pipeline Catches, What It Misses.

Prime Video's Off Campus, an adaptation of Elle Kennedy's The Deal (the first book of her thirteen-book Off Campus series), premiered twelve days ago. Amazon caught it cleanly. They optioned the series in October 2024, renewed for a second season in February on data confidence, and launched to a 93% Rotten Tomatoes score and #1 on JustWatch in five days. The trad pipeline did what the trad pipeline is built to do.

HBO Max's Heated Rivalry, an adaptation of Rachel Reid's book of the same name (the second book of her Game Changers series), aired six months earlier in late November 2025. The industry did not catch it the same way. By every signal that gets weighted heavily, the book looked midlist right up until the show forced the issue.

This is the first investigation in an ongoing series from Romance Signal. We're using the comparison between these two adaptations to walk through what the trad pipeline catches, what it misses, and where the signals worth reading actually live. Future volumes will continue the work.

The smaller audience was producing more written work per reader. Whether that pattern is readable as a leading indicator is the open question we're sitting with.

01 · What Goodreads Showed

Goodreads is the most important signal the romance industry reads. Rating counts, velocity, to-read lists, shelf activity. It's the public proxy for whether a book is finding an audience. Publishers acquire on it. Scouts watch it. Streamers price option terms against it.

For Elle Kennedy's Off Campus series, the signal was overwhelming. Nearly three million Goodreads ratings across the thirteen books before the show was cast. The Deal, the book the show adapts, crossed a million ratings on its own in November 2025. A decade of steady accumulation through BookTok-era romance discourse. Amazon renewed the show for a second season three months before it aired because the data already said the audience existed.

For Heated Rivalry, the signal was quiet. 34,000 Goodreads ratings two months before broadcast. Respectable for a six-year-old small-press romance. Unremarkable for an industry deciding whether to bet on an adaptation.

Off Campus shows fulfillment. Heated Rivalry looked midlist. The trad pipeline read each one accurately on the data it had access to. The question is whether more data existed somewhere else.

02 · What Reddit Showed

Reddit is a quieter signal than Goodreads. The romance industry watches r/RomanceBooks and r/Romantasy. Sometimes scouts read further. But dedicated subreddits for individual books are rare enough that when they form, they mean something.

r/heatedrivalry was founded in June 2025, five months before the HBO Max broadcast. Book readers started it on their own. They knew about the show but they were there for the book. By October, the subreddit had about a thousand members. By the time the show aired in late November, it had crossed five thousand. Two months later, fifty thousand.

The pre-broadcast signal was real but not staggering. A thousand subscribers in a niche subgenre is meaningful. It is also not the kind of number that overrides a 34,000-rating Goodreads picture. A trad scout looking at both would have weighted the Goodreads number more heavily, and that weighting wasn't wrong on its own terms. The Reddit signal was telling a real story at a volume the trad pipeline doesn't routinely listen to.

03 · What AO3 Was Saying

This is where the picture changes.

The Shane Hollander / Ilya Rozanov pairing tag on AO3 held 362 works in July 2025. Small in absolute terms. But the tag had been growing slowly and consistently for years. The community producing the work had organized itself in author networks, fic-rec aggregators, and Tumblr tags long before the show was a real possibility. When something on AO3 sustains a small but durable production rate over years rather than spiking and dying, that pattern means something.

Today, six months after broadcast, the tag holds 31,769 works. 98% of the corpus was written after the show aired. 5,200 new works per month against a pre-broadcast baseline of 52. The community that produced this volume of writing didn't appear in November 2025. It was already there. It just became visible at scale.

What kinds of stories take off on AO3? Most analysis misses the true magic of romance. Some books wrap up neatly and let you go. Others refuse to leave you. You turn the final page and realize you just want to move in.

The genre's writing about itself talks about heat level, trope, subgenre. It talks less about arcs. The romance arrives. The HEA lands. The question is what the story does on its way there, and what it leaves behind.

A closed arc ties everything off. Character, relationship, and narrative arcs all resolve. The reader experiences total resolution, a beautifully completed circle. These are the romances that hit every beat you came for. They take you on the journey you love and deliver you back, satisfied, ready to do it all again with the next book. Most romance is this. Most successful romance is this. It is what the genre is built around.

An open arc leaves room. The story intentionally or unintentionally invites what if. It alludes to critical details rather than spelling them out. The world stretches beyond the final page. There's a lingering echo, an invitation for the reader's imagination to co-create the characters' futures long after the book is shut.

The Deal and the wider Off Campus series are closed-arc all the way through. Thirteen books, thirteen complete love stories. Each one lands and resolves. The 120 AO3 works across the entire series is what closed-arc fulfillment looks like in fic production. The Garrett/Hannah pairing, the central couple of The Deal and the show, has 146 works total. Most are concentrated in the first two weeks after premiere. Audience showed up, read the book, loved it, and mostly didn't make anything with it. Because the book finished itself.

Heated Rivalry is open-arc. By the end of The Long Game, Shane and Ilya are fully out, in Shane's hometown. There's a clear HEA on the page. But the book also closes on Shane absorbing the traumatic breakdown of his team and nearly being forced to switch franchises, and that wound doesn't get tied off. What he feared would happen, happened. Readers are still arguing about whether it's actually a happy ending. That argument, plus the endless engine of fanfiction trying to answer it, is the open arc working as designed.

What if, from inside Heated Rivalry

It's implied Shane's coming out went alright, but were there elements of the team turning against him earlier on?

What if Shane's Asian heritage was spelled out more? Or what if we spent more time analyzing his assimilation as a means to blend in?

What if Shane and Ilya weren't rivals, would they still be inevitable? How crucial is the competitive element to who they become?

What if Shane had sent that text. Would Ilya have run after him, or was he too deep in his denial?

Those are the gaps an open arc leaves on purpose, or by happy accident. Each one is an invitation for a reader to keep writing. The open arc is the structural feature that produces the kind of sustained AO3 activity Heated Rivalry showed pre-broadcast. It is not the only kind of property AO3 tracks, and the open/closed distinction is one note inside a larger pipeline question. But it is a useful note. AO3's leading-indicator value, if it has one, is most likely strongest for open-arc work.

04 · The Pipeline Gap

Putting the three signals together, the picture for Heated Rivalry in October 2025 looked like this. 34,000 Goodreads ratings (quiet). A thousand-member subreddit founded by book readers (small but real). A pairing tag on AO3 with 362 works showing sustained durable growth over years (small in absolute terms but doing something unusual per reader).

The trad pipeline weighted Goodreads heavily, as it usually does. Reddit and AO3 were available to anyone who knew where to look, but reading them is different from acting on them. A scout who noticed 362 works for Shane and Ilya in July 2025 might have flagged it as interesting and still not justified betting the farm on it. The numbers were small. The pattern required knowing what slow durable growth on AO3 typically signals. That knowledge isn't standard equipment in trad acquisitions. The weighting wasn't unreasonable on its own terms.

But Heated Rivalry's actual readership signal lived on AO3. That is where the audience producing the post-broadcast detonation had been waiting, organizing, and writing for years. By the time Goodreads caught up, the show had already aired and the bet was already settled.

Off Campus is the other half of this picture. Closed-arc fulfillment, caught cleanly by Goodreads, with very little AO3 activity because the form doesn't generate it. The trad pipeline reads closed-arc work well. It is the pipeline's home turf. The question is not whether the pipeline works (it does, for what it sees). The question is what the pipeline isn't built to see, and whether the signals living outside it can be read carefully enough to matter.

The open question. Can AO3 (and adjacent community-network signals) be read as a leading indicator of crossover for open-arc properties before the trad pipeline catches up? And if it can be read, can it be read ethically? AO3 is a community space explicitly resistant to commercial extraction. The fact that information sits in public doesn't mean it should be harvested at scale for commercial decisions. Reading 362 works in a tag by hand to think about a research question is different from running automated pulls to feed acquisition models. The community's norms about how this work should be treated matter, and any leading-indicator method that ignores them isn't actually a method we should build.

We don't know the answers yet. We're working on both halves. There are other variables that could be doing some of the work in the crossover question, chemistry, trope architecture, source-material fidelity, the cultural moment around queer masculinity, casting, platform positioning. The eventual model probably combines several. But AO3 sustained durable activity in a small pairing tag is the most under-read signal in the picture. It's also the one with the most complicated ethics. Both of those things are true, and the work has to hold both.

The closed/open arc lens helps explain what AO3 catches. It is not the whole picture, and it is not the only variable that matters for crossover. It is one useful frame on a pipeline gap that future volumes will keep working through. We aren't selling a predictive model. We are walking through what the data already tells us, naming what it doesn't, and publishing the work as we go.

About this project

Romance Signal

Romance Signal is the research wing of Threnne. Built for romance, built for writers trying to understand what works.

Writers want to know what makes a romance succeed. Publishers, producers, scouts, and agents read signals to make those calls. We're publishing those signals, plus the harder work of finding the ones the trad pipeline misses, so writers can read their own work against what the data actually says.

This volume tests one variable. Upcoming volumes will test others. Chemistry, trope architecture, source-material fidelity, the cultural moment around queer masculinity, casting, tone, spice level. The eventual model will combine several. The pacing depends on what the data lets us examine cleanly.
