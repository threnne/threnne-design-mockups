Background

how a romance book gets acquired, and how it gets scouted for screen.

Two pipelines, same romance manuscript. Different decision-makers, different metrics, different timelines. This page maps both, so writers and industry readers can see what the institutional landscape actually looks like.

Romance Signal's published research investigates specific patterns inside this landscape. This page is the reference. Underlying mechanics, decision-makers, timelines, and signal weights, drawn from named 2026 industry sources and grounded in how the genre actually moves through both pipelines. Romance is its own market and its own ecosystem. The patterns described here are read with that in mind.

01

Pipeline at a glance

Both pipelines start from a manuscript. They diverge immediately on who reads first, what gets prioritized, and how long the path takes.

Publishing acquisition

Manuscript to bookstore

  1. 01
    Query
    Romance author submits to literary agent. Most agents in romance are open to direct query but require comp titles and platform.
  2. 02
    Submission
    Agent shops to 8-12 romance editors at Big Five imprints and category houses (Avon, Berkley, St. Martin's, Forever, Carina).
  3. 03
    P&L build
    Editor models margins against romance comp sales, BookTok presence, and series potential. Romance P&Ls weight backlist heavily.
  4. 04
    Pub board
    Cross-functional weekly vote including editor-in-chief, sales, marketing, production. Romance-specific imprints may have shorter approval chains.
  5. 05
    Offer & contract
    Advance against royalty, primary rights split, subsidiary rights including audio (high-value in romance) and translation.
  6. 06
    Edit & produce
    Twelve to eighteen months from contract to shelf. Romance has shorter cycles than literary fiction for time-to-market reasons.
  7. 07
    On-sale
    Bookstore launch coordinated with BookTok promotion windows and trope-tagged seasonal pushes.

Hollywood scouting

Manuscript to screen

  1. 01
    Manuscript surfaces
    Scout reader assigned, often before publication if BookTok momentum or auction heat is visible.
  2. 02
    Coverage
    Synopsis, cast positioning, set-piece highlights, heat-level note for romance specifically.
  3. 03
    IP memo
    Weekly briefing to producers and streamer execs. Romance properties flagged with subgenre and trope architecture.
  4. 04
    Producer pitch
    Match against client slate. Hallmark wants closed-arc warmth, streamers want spice and conflict, prestige wants literary romance.
  5. 05
    Option or shopping
    Twelve to eighteen month exclusive window. Romance options have moved faster in 2025-2026 with the streamer rush.
  6. 06
    Development
    Script, talent attachment, financing. Romance adaptations often hit attachment stage faster than other genres due to audience certainty.
  7. 07
    Greenlight to screen
    Fewer than ten percent of optioned books reach production. Romance closes that gap somewhat due to proven audience.

Publishing

In romance, the acquiring editor builds the case (P&L, comps, BookTok potential, audience match) and champions it to a weekly pub board including editor-in-chief, sales, marketing, and production. Romance imprints (Avon, Berkley, Forever) may have specialized board members.

Hollywood

Decisions sit with a single producer, studio executive, or streamer executive, briefed by a scout. For romance specifically, streamers (Netflix, Prime, Hallmark+) have become the dominant buyers post-2024.

Writer takeaway

Different rooms, different pitches. Publishing means surviving a cross-functional vote with romance-specific comps. Hollywood means pitching one buyer at a time, matched to their slate (Hallmark vs Netflix vs prestige needs different framings).

Publishing

Romance has a more accessible agent landscape than literary fiction, but Big Five access still requires representation. Some category romance houses accept unagented submissions; trade romance imprints generally do not.

Hollywood

Scouts deploy readers for synopsis, character, setting, set-piece, and heat-level coverage. BookTok momentum and pre-publication auction heat trigger scout attention earlier than for other genres.

Publishing

Romance is reduced to P&L with comp sales, return rates, BookTok presence, series potential, and audio rights value. Romance P&Ls weight backlist potential and audiobook revenue more heavily than other genres.

Hollywood

Scouts prioritize cinematic hook (the scene that sells the trailer), production viability (budget, casting feasibility), and slate fit (Hallmark vs Netflix vs prestige). For romance, the central couple's chemistry potential is load-bearing.

Writer takeaway

Editors need market proof. Scouts need screen proof. For romance, the scene that would open the trailer (the first meet, the trope moment, the chemistry beat) does double duty as the BookTok hook.

Publishing

Limited author platform, thin or stale romance comps, overpay risk in heated auctions, and unproven heat levels all raise concern. Subgenre saturation (eg. another standard hockey romance in 2026) also flags.

Hollywood

Expensive period settings (regency, fantasy realms), heavy CGI, aging cast arcs across long series, and voice-over-heavy interiority all create budget anxiety. Romance with fantasy elements (romantasy) faces extra production cost scrutiny.

Writer takeaway

Frame structural red flags with comparable success. Romantasy adaptations exist because Fourth Wing existed. The comp absorbs the risk argument.

Publishing

Romance editor read times vary by imprint. Hot manuscripts can close in weeks; standard submissions take three to nine months across the round.

Hollywood

For romance with visible BookTok signal or auction heat, scouts surface in twenty-four to seventy-two hours and options can lock pre-publication. Heated Rivalry and Icebreaker both moved this fast.

Writer takeaway

Have adaptation representation in place before submission. For romance specifically, this is increasingly standard because the Hollywood timeline now sometimes runs ahead of the publishing one.

Publishing

Romance advances are typically smaller than literary fiction for debut authors, but escalators, ebook rates, and audio royalties drive long-tail economics. Backlist matters more in romance than almost any other category.

Hollywood

Option fees are typically ten percent of an underlying purchase price. Purchase price often runs two to four percent of production budget. Episodic and franchise passives often outweigh option fees over time.

Writer takeaway

Negotiate compounding back-end terms. Romance backlist royalties and adaptation passives often exceed initial advances over a career.

Publishing

Romance auctions compressed in 2024-2026 with BookTok-driven momentum. Hot manuscripts can multiply advances in days. Most romance deals are still single-offer negotiations.

Hollywood

For romance specifically, creative vision and adaptation track record (Bridgerton, Reedsy) often beat top bids. Producers with proven romance pipelines (Shondaland, others) command setup advantages.

Writer takeaway

Take the strongest setup path, not just the highest headline. Romance adaptations live or die on producer execution. A producer with a romance track record beats a higher offer from a generalist.

03

Levers we're investigating

A short list of moves we think might shift outcomes for romance writers across both pipelines. We don't have settled evidence on most of these. This section names what we're testing, what would tell us it works, and what we don't yet know.

Both

Engineering a BookTok-shaped scene

What we're testing

Whether building one clip-ready scene per romance (the trope moment, the chemistry beat, the chapter ending that makes a screenshot) correlates with both BookTok pickup and scout coverage.

What the field shows

Icebreaker and Fourth Wing both had scenes that became BookTok moments before adaptation interest landed. The pattern is consistent enough to take seriously.

Where it's still open

What makes a scene BookTok-shaped versus just dramatic isn't well understood. We suspect emotional escalation, dialogue density, and trope legibility matter, but we don't have enough comparative data to know what's necessary versus sufficient.

Hollywood

Short-form as adaptation pitch

What we're testing

Whether publishing romance novellas, short stories, or webtoon collaborations at high concept density gets faster scout attention than full novels.

What the field shows

Scouts increasingly read short-form content because it's faster to evaluate than a hundred-thousand-word manuscript. Microdrama and webtoon adaptations have become reference points in 2025-2026.

Where it's still open

It's unclear whether short-form actually shortens the path to adaptation, or whether it's a pitch surface that still requires a novel to anchor the deal. We're watching whether romance writers with strong short-form portfolios convert at higher rates.

Both

Backlist re-detonation

What we're testing

Whether building rediscoverable moments in romance backlist (scenes, tropes, characters that can re-surface on BookTok years after publication) creates durable career value.

What the field shows

The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo is the clearest recent example. Published in 2017, then rediscovered on BookTok in 2021, it had logged 37 weeks on the NYT bestseller list by the end of that year and crossed a million copies sold by early 2022, with Netflix picking up the film adaptation soon after. The book didn't change; the discovery mechanism did.

Where it's still open

We don't know what makes a romance backlist title rediscoverable versus forgettable. Open-arc structure looks load-bearing per Volume 001, but it's one variable among many. The next several volumes will keep testing this.

Publishing

Subgenre comp stacks

What we're testing

Whether building submission comp stacks that stay tightly inside the romance subgenre (matching trope, heat level, audience profile) outperforms stacks that reach across categories.

What the field shows

Editors weight comp sales heavily in P&L modeling. Inside-genre comps reduce the explanatory burden on the submission and let the editor's case to the pub board be cleaner.

Where it's still open

What we don't know is whether comp tightness matters at the edges. A romance with a literary fiction crossover ambition might benefit from a mixed stack; a category romance probably benefits from staying narrow. The threshold isn't clear.

Both

Owned audience as primary asset

What we're testing

Whether building a newsletter audience (rather than social following) functions as the more durable career asset for romance writers across both pipelines.

What the field shows

Newsletters travel with the writer across platform changes. Social followings often don't. Editors and scouts both report watching mailing list size as a stability signal more than viral spikes.

Where it's still open

We don't have good data on how mailing list size correlates with either pipeline's decisions. The intuition is widely shared, but the threshold at which a list starts mattering for romance acquisitions specifically isn't documented.
